What a desktop appraisal is

A desktop appraisal is a full written appraisal report completed without a site visit. I research public records, the closed comparable sales, and the local market as of the effective date. Each report includes a detailed property description, a market conditions section with charts, a written explanation of why each comparable was selected and how each adjustment was derived, a location map, photographs of the property and the comparables, and the public record pages. Each report is USPAP compliant and typically runs 30–40 pages.

Why a desktop appraisal is credible

The research and analysis are the same as an appraisal with a site visit, and it reaches the same value conclusion.

On what an appraisal inspection actually is — the Appraisal Institute calls it an observation rather than an inspection, and that's the more accurate word. When I walk a home I'm looking at size, layout, quality of finish, condition, and what's been updated. I'm not testing systems, opening panels, or getting on the roof the way a home inspector would. It's a visual observation and it's cursory by design — which is why photos from you can do the same job.

There's a second reason it works, and it's the part most people don't think about. The comparable sales I analyze aren't inspected either — I've never been inside them. What I have for each one is the listing photos and the agent's description. So when I use your photos and your description of the property, I'm comparing like with like: photos and descriptions on both sides of the analysis. Inspecting the subject alone would actually create an asymmetry, since I'd know it in more detail than any of the sales I'm measuring it against.

Why I recommend the desktop

For the land, a site visit adds very little. Vacant land value turns on size, zoning, access, utilities, and topography, and those are verified from public records, parcel maps, and aerial imagery — the same way the comparable land sales are analyzed.

For the two homes, a visit would mainly add cost and scheduling without changing the conclusion. Good photos give me everything I'd gather standing there myself, and you'll have a chance to read each report and ask me to revise it if I've misread condition or quality anywhere.

Photographs

For the homes, wide shots from a corner of each room work better than close-ups, along with the exterior and anything updated or in need of repair. If photos aren't available, a general description of the condition works too. Something like “completely renovated with high quality finishes” or “dated but well maintained” tells me most of what I need, and I can fill in the rest from what's available online. No photos are needed for the land.
