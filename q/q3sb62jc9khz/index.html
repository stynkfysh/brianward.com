You have the right idea, and both names you used are for the same thing. A “date of death appraisal” and a “step-up appraisal” are the same report — a retrospective appraisal with the effective date set to the day your sister passed, rather than today. Everything in it is researched as of that date: the comparable sales, the market conditions, and the value conclusion. Selling next year doesn't change any of that, so getting this done now while the records are fresh is exactly the right order to do things in.

These make up about half of my practice — I've prepared thousands of them — and I also serve regularly as an expert witness in litigation, divorce, and mediation matters, so this is very familiar ground.

One thing I'll need from you before anything can be ordered: the date your sister passed away. That single date sets the effective date of the entire appraisal.

On the property itself — three units on one lot is squarely within my license. I'm certified for one-to-four unit residential, and I appraise the property as a single three-unit residential property, the way the local market actually trades these. Why I recommend the desktop

The analysis behind both options is identical, and both reach the same value conclusion. The difference is whether I take the photographs or you do.

It's worth knowing what an appraisal inspection actually is. The Appraisal Institute calls it an observation rather than an inspection, and that's the more accurate word. When I walk a property I'm looking at size, layout, quality of finish, condition, and what's been updated. I'm not testing systems, opening panels, or getting on the roof the way a home inspector would. It's visual and cursory by design — which is why photos from you can do the same job. Wide shots from a corner of each room in each unit, the exterior from the street and from the rear, and anything recently updated or in need of repair give me the same information I'd gather standing there myself.

There's a second reason it works, and it's the part most people don't think about. The comparable sales I analyze aren't inspected either — I've never been inside them. All I have for each one is the listing photos and the agent's description. So when I use your photos and your description, I'm comparing like with like, photos and descriptions on both sides of the analysis. Inspecting your property alone would actually create an imbalance, since I'd know it in more detail than any of the sales I'm measuring it against.

There's a practical point too: you live in one of the three units and the other two presumably have tenants. The desktop means no one has to be scheduled around or let into their home.

The one situation where I'd steer you to the standard instead is if the property is being divided among several heirs. It doesn't change the value — but at a sensitive time, the added thoroughness of an in-person observation helps everyone feel confident in the report. If that's your situation, let me know.
