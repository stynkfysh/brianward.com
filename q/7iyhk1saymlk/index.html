Why a desktop appraisal works here

Neither option involves a visit to the condo, and for this assignment I don't think one would add anything. The Appraisal Institute calls an appraiser's site visit an observation rather than an inspection, and that's the more accurate word. When I walk a property I'm looking at size, layout, quality of finish, condition, and what's been updated — I'm not testing systems or opening panels the way a home inspector would. It's visual and cursory by design, which is why photos from you can do the same job. And with the date of death nearly a year back, walking through the condo today would only show me how it looks now, not how it was on November 29, 2025.

There's a second reason it works, and it's the part most people don't think about. The comparable sales I analyze aren't inspected either — I've never been inside them. What I have for each one is the listing photos and the agent's description. So when I use your photos and your description of the condo, I'm comparing like with like: photos and descriptions on both sides of the analysis.

A desktop is not an automated estimate. I select the comparable sales, make and support every adjustment, and sign the report myself as a California Certified Residential Appraiser.

Why I recommend the Desktop

Photographs

Ideally you'd send me photos of the condo, particularly any from around November 2025. Wide shots from a corner of each room work better than close-ups.

If you don't have photos from that period, a general description of the condition it was in works too. Something like “completely renovated with high quality finishes” or “dated but well maintained” tells me most of what I need, and I can fill in the rest from what's available online.

I see the condo was listed for sale this past summer, so those listing photos will help as well. If anything was updated between November 2025 and the listing, let me know so I can leave it out.
