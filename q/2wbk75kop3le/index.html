Why a desktop appraisal is credible

The Appraisal Institute calls an appraiser's site visit an observation rather than an inspection, and that's the more accurate word. When I walk a property I'm looking at size, layout, quality of finish, condition, and what's been updated. I'm not testing systems, opening panels, or getting on the roof the way a home inspector would. It's a visual observation and it's cursory by design, which is why photos from you can do the same job. Deferred maintenance affects value through what a typical buyer would see and expect to fix, and that's exactly what good photos show.

There's a second reason it works, and it's the part most people don't think about. The comparable sales I analyze aren't inspected either. I've never been inside them. What I have for each one is the listing photos and the agent's description. So when I use your photos and your description of the townhouse, I'm comparing like with like: photos and descriptions on both sides of the analysis. Inspecting the townhouse alone would actually create an asymmetry, since I'd know it in more detail than any of the sales I'm measuring it against.

A desktop is not an automated estimate or an agent's opinion of price. I select the comparable sales, make and support every adjustment, and sign the report as a California Certified Residential Appraiser, and it's USPAP compliant. The report states that the condition is based on the photos and information provided, so anyone reading it can see exactly what the value rests on. I'm the one who does the appraisal work myself, start to finish, and I regularly serve as an expert witness in family law matters — my reports are written to hold up when more than one person is reading them closely.

Photographs

Ideally you'd send me photos of the townhouse. Wide shots from a corner of each room work better than close-ups: the kitchen, each bathroom and bedroom, the living and dining areas, the laundry area, and the garage, along with the front and rear of the unit and any patio.

For the deferred maintenance, two photos of each item work best: one wide enough to show where it is, and one close enough to show the problem. A short list of what needs work helps too, and if you have any contractor estimates, send those along — they tell me more about the cost of a repair than a walk-through would.

If photos aren't practical, a general description of the condition works. Something like “dated but well maintained, except for water damage on the hall bath ceiling and worn carpet throughout” tells me most of what I need. Since this is a buyout, it can help if everyone involved agrees on the photos and the list of repair items before you send them, so the starting point isn't up for debate later.
