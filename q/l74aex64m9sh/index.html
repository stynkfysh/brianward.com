A date-of-death appraisal is a retrospective valuation: it tells you what the home was worth on the date of passing, using the sales and market conditions from that time rather than today's. Date-of-death appraisals make up roughly half of my practice, and I've prepared thousands of them.

For this assignment I recommend a desktop appraisal. Below are the two desktop options, how the process works, and why a desktop is a credible way to value this home. The one thing I need from you to set this up correctly is the date of death, since that becomes the effective date of the appraisal.

Why a desktop appraisal is credible

The Appraisal Institute calls an appraisal inspection an observation rather than an inspection, and that's the more accurate word. When I walk a property I'm looking at size, layout, quality of finish, condition, and what's been updated. I'm not testing systems, opening panels, or getting on the roof the way a home inspector would. It's a visual observation and it's cursory by design, which is why photos and a description from you can do the same job.

There's a second reason it works, and it's the part most people don't think about. The comparable sales I analyze aren't inspected either. I've never been inside them. What I have for each one is the listing photos and the agent's description. So when I use your photos and your description of the home, I'm comparing like with like: photos and descriptions on both sides of the analysis. Inspecting the home alone would actually create an asymmetry, since I'd know it in more detail than any of the sales I'm measuring it against.

For a retrospective appraisal there's one more point. A walk-through today shows the home as it is now, not as it was on the date of death. What matters is its condition on that date, which photos and your description from that period capture better than a visit would.

Why I recommend the Desktop

The value conclusion is the same with either desktop option, and an in-person Standard appraisal wouldn't change it either; I don't think it buys you anything here. The difference between the two desktops is how much of the supporting analysis is written out on the page. The Desktop shows its work: why each sale was chosen, how each adjustment was supported, and what the Palm Springs market was doing at the time. That written support is what a tax preparer, attorney, or reviewer is most likely to want on file, and it's why the Desktop is my recommendation for date-of-death work.

Photographs

Ideally you'd send me photos of the home, particularly any from close to the date of death. Wide shots from a corner of each room work better than close-ups, along with the exterior from the street and the rear. If you don't have photos from that period, a general description of the condition it was in works too. Something like "completely renovated with high quality finishes" or "dated but well maintained" tells me most of what I need, and I can fill in the rest from what's available online. It also helps to know about any updates made after the date of death, so I can leave them out of the value.
